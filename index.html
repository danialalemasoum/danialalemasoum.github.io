From uncalibrated empathy to systemic bias

The AI Multiplier Effect in public service design

Research question

When we design public services with empathy, and then build AI on top of those designs, what happens to the people our empathy missed?

Abstract

Public service design relies on empathy to keep services human. Empathy can be uncalibrated: tuned to one group, often the most visible one. When AI is built on such designs, the blind spot is encoded in data and decision logic and repeated at algorithmic scale. This work presents the concept, a three-layer lens (micro, meso, macro), a preliminary reading of five sources, and a draft design-stage instrument, the Bias-Aware AI Canvas.

Keywords: public service design · empathic design · AI bias · algorithmic scale · autonomy · Finland

Early-stage and conceptual. The canvas is a draft and untested.

Research

From uncalibrated empathy to systemic bias

Public service design relies on empathy to keep services human. My question is what happens to the people our empathy missed, once AI builds on those designs.

Uncalibrated empathy

Empathic design can lead designers to over-empathise with specific groups and neglect diverse communities (Hyökki et al., 2024, citing Vink & Oertzen, 2018). The designer feels they understand the user, but the understanding is tuned to one group, often the most visible one.

Calibration does not mean less empathy. It means knowing whose experience your empathy is built on.

The AI Multiplier Effect

A blind spot is built into a design process, encoded into the data and decision logic of an automated service, and then multiplied at algorithmic speed and scale. Before automation, a blind spot in a form affected the people who used that form. With AI, one local blind spot can become a structural pattern, and it is hard to see because each single decision looks reasonable.

Three layers where a blind spot can enter

Borrowing the micro, meso and macro levels of Trischler & Trischler (2022). The blind spots below are my hypotheses, informed by the literature. They are not findings.

Autonomy means being able to challenge a decision

By autonomy I mean a person's ability to notice, question and correct a decision that concerns them. Someone living far from the municipality or the first service point may find it much harder to put a wrong automated decision right. This is an illustration of an argument, not a research finding.

It follows that participation alone is not enough. Lydén et al. (2026) remind us that empowerment is embedded in power relations, so people must be able to define their own needs and to reach someone who can correct a decision.

Limits and next steps

Conceptual and preliminary. The canvas is untested, and the Lapland figures I use elsewhere are context, not evidence for the argument.

  1. A systematic literature review, to position the canvas against design-management and AI-fairness frameworks.
  2. A small simulated workshop with the participants I can reach.
  3. Later, co-design with the people affected, with informed consent and their authority respected.

    Three proposed values

    Co-creation

    Designers, policymakers and citizen representatives work together, and people define their own needs.

    Edge-case prioritisation

    Start from who is easiest to miss, across the micro, meso and macro layers.

    Algorithmic transparency

    Make data and decision logic visible and open to challenge.

    The canvas does not remove bias. Its job is to make hidden assumptions visible early, and open to discussion by the people affected.

    Refining algorithmic tools for European policing: the design of Euroduc

    Danial Alemasoum · MSc Digital Design, Amsterdam University of Applied Sciences (HvA), 2024–2025
    Research report from a team project, adapted for the web

    Abstract

    Police departments handle growing volumes of data, including criminal reports, CCTV footage and social media content, and manual review is increasingly impractical. This report asks how refining algorithms can improve the efficiency, speed and safety of police work while addressing the resistance to adopting new technologies in European law enforcement. A qualitative design-research approach combined an interview with a Swedish police officer, a task-based user test with a data analyst at a European security agency, a literature review, and an analysis of software for law enforcement and private security. Agencies appear to favour older, proven systems because of concerns about safety, privacy, reliability and retraining, and restricted hardware policies make data sharing a practical bottleneck. These insights shaped Euroduc, a prototype with e-commerce-inspired filtering and a collaborative Timeline with role-based access and traceable actions. The evidence base is small, so the findings are indicative rather than general.

    Keywords: smart policing · algorithmic transparency · design research · technology adoption · human-centred tools

    Introduction

    Police departments handle large and growing volumes of data. Reviewing it by hand takes considerable manpower, and time management is a recognised factor in crisis response (Police Executive Research Forum, 2022). Machine learning (ML) and natural language processing offer help in this area (Sarzaeim et al., 2023), yet many European agencies hesitate to adopt new technology.

    Research question. How can refining algorithms improve the efficiency, speed and safety of police work while addressing the resistance to adopting new technologies in European law enforcement agencies?

    The project has two parts: an inquiry into the barriers to adoption, and the design of Euroduc, a prototype that responds to what the inquiry found. Data collection was constrained. Several police innovation labs in Europe declined interview requests, citing security and privacy concerns.

    Related work

    Smart policing. Sarzaeim et al. (2023) review ML and NLP applications in policing, such as crime statistics analysis, predictive crime mapping and facial recognition. They stress that algorithmic models must give consistent and explainable results to support trust and accountability.

    Transparency and bias. Algopol (2020) highlights the risks of algorithmic bias and misuse of sensitive police data, and argues that AI judgements cannot be trusted when it is unclear how conclusions are reached. A framework for LLM-assisted smart policing (2024) tests models such as BART, GPT-3 and GPT-4 for crime prediction with zero-shot prompting, few-shot prompting and fine-tuning. It reports efficiency potential, but stresses fine-tuning to limit bias and the need for transparent, accountable algorithms.

    Barriers to adoption. Statewatch (2024) points to limited testing of new technologies, the potential for system failures and associated safety risks, and privacy and security risks linked to planned expansion of police data sharing in the European Union.

    Evidence and accountability. Meijer and Wessels (2019) review the benefits and drawbacks of predictive policing and conclude that current initiatives rest more on convincing arguments and anecdotal evidence than on systematic research: some studies report crime reductions and others no effect. Bennett Moses and Chan (2018) raise three issues, namely the assumptions built into algorithmic prediction, the lack of independent evaluation and the accountability gap in how decisions made with such tools can be justified. Rudin (2019) argues that for high-stakes decisions, inherently interpretable models should be preferred over explaining black-box models afterwards.

    Practitioners' stance. Sandhu and Fussey (2021) studied how UK police work with predictive tools and found that many officers understand the limits of the input data, are sceptical, and keep exercising their own judgement. This is consistent with the hesitation reported by the interviewed officer.

    Interaction precedents. Platforms with very large inventories, such as Freepik, Temu and Shein, manage them with simple tagging and filtering. This pattern informed the search design.

    Method

    The approach follows design research, in which an artefact is designed and evaluated for its utility (Hevner et al., 2004). Three methods were combined and compared against each other (Figure 1).

    Interview

    One interview with a Swedish police officer with more than eight years of experience, who asked to remain anonymous. Prepared questions guided a discussion of the efficiency of current police algorithms, areas for improvement and challenges in policing technology.

    Task-based user test

    A group session with a data analyst at a European security agency. The team prepared a case-solving game (A Solved Case, Criminal Case Games) and asked the analyst to work through it using the prototype's labelling and timeline tools. The aim was not to solve the case but to observe how the tools were used and whether users needed them.

    Literature and market analysis

    A review of academic and policy sources on algorithmic policing, and an analysis of companies developing software for law enforcement and private security.

    Ethical considerations

    The main concerns were algorithmic bias, misuse of sensitive police data and transparency. Collected data was anonymised and stored securely.

    Findings

    Preference for proven systems

    Agencies tend to rely on older systems because they are proven safe and reliable. The interviewed officer noted that ML is rarely used and gave the example of ATMS, which often use outdated but dependable software. Moving to new technology brings the risk of failures and the need for extensive retraining, which costs time and resources. Concerns that ML could reinforce existing biases and raise privacy risks add to the hesitation.

    Data sharing as a hidden bottleneck

    The officer described how restrictions on USB sticks and other portable hardware force officers to print data, which makes it hard to share information quickly with colleagues. This led to the Timeline feature.

    User test

    The analyst started with labelling, which worked well: he could categorise all the data he needed and remove irrelevant material. The Timeline was welcomed for sharing, but he stressed that hierarchy is important, since no tool allowed restricting the next officer from seeing certain notes.

    Literature

    The sources agree on one design requirement: AI output cannot be trusted when its reasoning is opaque. Transparency and explainability therefore became design principles.

    Design: Euroduc

    Filtering. Officers filter material by action (for example walking or running), by presence of people and by presence of vehicles, using simple toggles. For a robbery, an officer can reduce thousands of surveillance clips to those where a suspect is seen running.

    Timeline. Officers drag footage, text reports and videos into a chronological structure, add personal notes, restrict access to some items and share securely inside the software. The next officer sees the steps already taken, which supports handover.

    Principles. Transparency: filtering actions and results are logged and traceable, so officers can see why results appear. Bias mitigation: filters use objective criteria such as movement patterns and environmental context, not demographics, race or personal identifiers. Security: the tool runs inside a secure internal network, avoiding USB transfers and third-party cloud tools.

    Iterations after testing. Role-based access control, a hierarchical Timeline structure, and a user-defined tagging system so that tags can be adapted to different investigations.

    Interactive exercise: filter and audit

    This exercise mimics the Euroduc filter on invented data. It shows a trade-off that the design has to manage: narrower filters speed up review but can hide relevant material, which is why every filter is logged and reversible.

    Find the suspect

    Invented scenario: a robbery was reported and witnesses say the suspect fled on foot. Sixteen clips come from nearby cameras. Ground truth is shown here only for the exercise. In a real case it is not known.

    Discussion

    The findings support a cautious reading: refining systems that officers already trust is a more practical and arguably more ethical route than replacing them. It limits retraining, reduces the risk of failure and keeps accountability visible. For practice, the report recommends workshops and clear evidence of benefits to address resistance, robust testing, collaboration between analysts, researchers and developers, and algorithms that are transparent, unbiased and privacy-respecting.

    The resistance can also be read through technology-acceptance research. Venkatesh et al. (2003) identify performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence and facilitating conditions as determinants of acceptance and use. The retraining burden maps onto effort expectancy, and hardware restrictions onto facilitating conditions. This mapping is my interpretation and was not tested in the study.

    Limitations

    The practitioner evidence is one interview and one user test, with participants from two organisations, so the findings are not representative. Efficiency and speed gains were not measured. The bias-mitigation and security principles are design intentions that have not been validated. The prototype was not tested in an operational setting. Perspectives of policymakers and technology developers are missing and would strengthen future work. Meijer and Wessels (2019) call for independent tests of the expectations attached to policing technology, and the same applies to Euroduc.

    Conclusion

    Optimised algorithms can improve data handling and decision-making in policing, but safety concerns, resistance to change and retraining needs have to be addressed first. Euroduc responds with an e-commerce-inspired filter and a Timeline that supports collaboration within security restrictions. Testing with practitioners produced concrete refinements, namely role-based access, hierarchical organisation and custom tags.

    OPEN HOUSE: designing emotional connection through interactive narratives

    Danial Alemasoum · Research report in response to the Playground Emerging Interactions brief
    Installation presented and tested in Amsterdam

    Abstract

    Digital communication often prioritises efficiency over intimacy. OPEN HOUSE is an immersive, participatory installation that asks how environments can mediate authentic emotional connection beyond image and text. Two participants each upload up to ten short videos expressing what “feeling at home” means to them. The videos are processed in TouchDesigner: each participant gets a personal wall, and a central wall merges 2–5 second snippets from both into one evolving narrative. A keyboard of colour-coded buttons lets participants change the floor's ambient colour. The installation was tested with 20 students aged 20 to 28 through interviews, surveys and observation. Testing showed that some participants did not know which buttons to press, missed the floor colour change, struggled to upload videos, or did not grasp the concept, which points to the need for onboarding cues. The report positions the work against related installations and outlines scaling to more participants and public spaces.

    Keywords: interactive installation · emotional connection · co-creation · narrative · participatory design

    Introduction

    Everything online moves fast, feels shallow and leaves many people tired. OPEN HOUSE responds to the Playground Emerging Interactions brief, which calls for new modes of human resonance and shared connection. Rather than an interface or an app, it offers a space in which people can slow down and be present together without having to perform or explain anything.

    Research question. How can we create environments that mediate authentic emotional connection, beyond image and text?

    The concept of “home” was chosen because it is open: it can be a person, a colour, a place, a sound, a smell or a fleeting moment. Because the installation was presented in Amsterdam, a deeply multicultural city, this openness let each participant bring a personal version of home into the space.

    Related work and theoretical frame

    Two installations served as reference points (Table 1). Connected Worlds (Design I/O) is large and playful, with children moving through projected environments whose ecosystem responds to their actions. The VC art installation (Inavate, 2023) links people in different cities through synchronised video screens. OPEN HOUSE keeps the idea of co-creation but trades motion for memory: interaction is reflective, not physical.

    Four sources shaped the design. Cohn (2013) shows that people can interpret fragmented or shuffled images using mental models and context, which supports an unfixed, randomised narrative. Sanders and Stappers (2008) treat co-creation as a way for people to shape meaning rather than only consume it. Lynch (1960) describes the city as a collection of mental images, and the installation collects personal definitions of home in a similar way. McCarthy and Wright (2004) argue that meaningful interaction is an experience, not mere use: OPEN HOUSE was designed to be felt.

    Further work frames the intent. Hassenzahl (2010) centres design on the experiences technology makes possible. Hallnäs and Redström (2001) propose slow technology, designed for reflection, which fits the aim of slowing people down. Gaver et al. (2003) argue that ambiguity can be a resource for design, giving people room for their own interpretation, which supports the open-ended, non-linear structure. Elliot and Maier (2014) review research on how perceiving colour relates to psychological functioning, which is the background to the colour-coded buttons.

    Design

    Participants first pass three posters along the path to the interaction space, which introduce home as something fluid. They then scan separate QR codes and upload up to ten short videos about what feeling at home means to them. In the room, each participant has a personal wall that continuously shows their videos, and a central wall merges snippets of 2–5 seconds from both participants using a randomised editing logic. At the centre, an interaction station with colour-coded buttons (for example red, blue and green) changes the floor's ambient colour at any moment. The experience is non-linear and open-ended, and participants stay as long as they wish.

    Evaluation

    The installation was tested in its fully set-up form, with every stage running, from uploading videos to co-watching the evolving narrative. Twenty students from various academic backgrounds, all studying at universities in Amsterdam, took part. Ages ranged from 20 to 28. About 50% identified as female, 30% as male, and the rest preferred not to say (Figure 4).

    Data came from short interviews and online surveys with prepared questions on clarity, emotional engagement and ease of interaction, plus open comments. Participants were told that their behaviour in the room would be observed for research purposes, and no recordings were made.

    Findings

    Testing challenged some design assumptions. Several participants did not know which buttons to press or did not notice the floor colour change. Others had difficulty uploading videos, or said the idea of “feeling at home” had not come across. One participant asked, “What's the point? Is it reminding me of my memories?”. These comments reveal a gap between the intended emotional depth and the experienced one.

    The open-endedness suited some participants, while others needed a little more structure. Observation also showed the other side: some people became absorbed and stayed in the space for 20–30 minutes, simply watching.

    Ethics and privacy

    Trust was treated as part of the experience design. Participants were asked not to upload harmful or explicit material, all videos were screened manually, and all personal data is deleted permanently after the experience. The aim was the opposite of attention-harvesting design: people need to feel safe to share something real.

    Interactive exercise: compose the shared wall

    The central wall blends snippets of 2–5 seconds from the participants' videos using a randomised logic. This exercise simulates one minute of that wall. It also asks a question the report leaves open: what happens to intimacy when more than two people share the wall?

    Discussion and reflection

    Ambiguity was a deliberate choice (Gaver et al., 2003). There was no start screen, no end credits and no interface telling people what to do. This invited curiosity, though some participants did not realise they could interact more deeply, so gentle guidance through sound, light transitions or soft cues is planned.

    Scaling is the main open question. With three, four or more participants, could the central wall remix more narratives and still keep emotional intimacy? Could the work move into public spaces such as libraries, metro stations and campuses, where strangers pass each other every day without really seeing one another? The simulator above shows only the arithmetic of dilution, not the experience, and testing is needed to answer it.

    The sample was 20 students in one city, so the findings do not generalise, and the evaluation relied on self-report and observation without recordings.

    Conclusion

    OPEN HOUSE proposes that design does not always need to be loud or efficient to be powerful. Slowing people down, giving them permission to reflect and letting their memories meet can create a small shared pause. Testing showed where that intention was not yet carried by the space, and it set a clear direction for the next iteration.
